# All About Pivoting

[Video Link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8pNxKX1SUGE)

What Is a Pivot

* A pivot simply means **changing your idea**.
* At the earliest stages (pre-launch or just after launch), changing ideas and assumptions rapidly in quick succession is normal and should feel **lightweight**.
* Moving slowly without changing assumptions when an idea fails is a sign you are **doing it wrong**.

Why You Should Pivot

* **Opportunity cost:** You can only work on one startup idea at a time; sticking to something that is not working prevents you from working on an alternative that could.
* **Rule of thumb equation:** If `(how well things are working / months of full-time concerted effort) < (excitement to work on something else + confidence you can find something better)`, you should pivot.
* Working on an idea for **many months with no traction** is the strongest signal to pivot.
* Pivoting grants **more shots on goal**; executing multiple high-quality iterations to complete, ship, and deliver products dramatically increases your statistical odds of finding product-market fit.

Good Reasons to Pivot

* You **hate working on the idea**.
* It is **not growing** despite sustained effort.
* You realize you have **poor founder-market fit** and are not the right person to build this product.
* You are relying on an **external factor outside your control** for success (e.g., waiting for mainstream VR hardware adoption or crypto market surges).
* You are completely **out of ideas** on how to make the current product work.
* You **know in your heart it is not going to work**, even if you attempt to maintain appearances to investors and the outside world.

Bad Reasons to Pivot

* Trying to **escape hard work**, such as pivoting right when development ends to avoid doing sales.
* Being a **chronic ideator** who jumps ideas constantly without ever completing, launching, or following through.
* Chasing a **hot trend** simply because you read about a funding round on TechCrunch.

Why Founders Wait Too Long to Pivot

* **Loss aversion:** Founders overvalue what they have already invested and struggle to walk away.
* **The "uncanny valley" trap of a little bit of traction:** Having a handful of users or one customer keeps founders stuck in limbo; outright failure is actually an advantage because it allows founders to declare immediate bankruptcy on the idea without regrets.
* **Confusing politeness with traction:** Potential customers rarely say an idea is terrible; they politely request more features, leading founders into endless product iteration loops without real intent to buy.
* **Fear of admitting defeat** or blaming failure on customers or investors rather than the idea itself.
* **Over-relying on perseverance anecdotes:** Believing that stubborn persistence alone works, which is statistically equivalent to treating the lottery as an actionable strategy.

Evaluating and Choosing a Pivot Idea

* Choose an idea that makes you **more excited and optimistic** to wake up in the morning, rather than settling for uninspiring ideas (like ad tech) that nobody cares about.
* Counter-intuitively, picking an idea perceived as **harder or more ambitious** often works better because it inspires founders and customers.
* Pick an idea that you can **build and validate quickly**, rather than pivoting into an idea that demands years of research and development before shipping.
* Understand whether your idea requires **venture capital scale** (e.g., potential for hundreds of millions or billions in net revenue, scalable within 5–10 years, software margins of 70–80%, potential to be a public company).

Idea Quality Score Framework (Rank 1 to 10)

* **How big the idea seems:** Is it clear how this could become a large, standalone or publicly traded company?
* **Founder-market fit:** Are you and your co-founders uniquely qualified to build and sell this specific product based on past experience or domain expertise?
* **Ease of getting started:** Can you build a prototype and launch an MVP quickly without needing massive upfront capital or hiring external specialists?
* **Early market feedback:** Do target customers immediately want the product and close straightforwardly, or does the market actively resist buying?

Pivoting Mechanics and Warnings

* **Avoid whiplash:** Pivoting too frequently (e.g., daily or weekly) burns out founders; losing morale and becoming depressed kills the company faster than working on a mediocre idea.
* **Do not pivot with employees:** Changing direction with a team of employees slows down execution and ruins team morale; keep the team small and only hire after the idea is proven to work.
* **Take founder accountability:** Do not rely on external authority figures or investors to tell you whether to pivot or give up; the decision is entirely on the founder.
