# How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas

Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Th8JoIan4dg

Common Mistakes with Startup Ideas

* **Solution in search of a problem (SISP):** Starting with a technology (e.g., "AI is cool, what can I apply it to?") leads to **superficially plausible, made-up problems** that users do not actually care about.
* **Abstract societal problems:** Broad issues (e.g., global poverty) are real, but they are **too abstract and intractable** as starting points for a startup.
* **Tar pit ideas:** Widespread, recurring ideas that many founders encounter (e.g., an app to coordinate weekend plans with friends) that seem simple to build, but have **hidden structural barriers** that cause nearly all attempts to fail.
* **Failing to Google prior attempts:** Founders often fail to research who has already attempted their exact idea, why they failed, and what the **fundamentally hard structural challenge** is.
* **The false binary of ideas:** Avoid latching onto the very first concept without analysis, but do not wait for a perfect idea either; treat an idea as a **plausible starting point** that will morph over time.

Framework to Evaluate Startup Ideas (10 Key Questions)

* **Founder-market fit:** The most critical question—is your specific team uniquely qualified to execute this? Look for an idea that is **uniquely good for your specific team**, not a "good idea" in the abstract.
* **Market size:** The market must be **either already massive ($1B+) or small but rapidly growing** (e.g., Bitcoin in 2012).
* **Acute problem:** The best problems are urgent pain points where the alternative to your solution is **literally nothing**.
* **Presence of competition:** Counter-intuitively, competition is a **positive validation of market demand**; in crowded spaces, you simply need a **specific new insight** that incumbents missed.
* **Personal desire:** If neither you nor anyone in your immediate network needs the product, there is a high risk that **nobody wants it**.
* **Recent changes in the world:** The best timing occurs when a problem has **only recently become possible or necessary** due to technological breakthroughs, regulatory changes, or macro shifts (e.g., on-demand delivery creating the need for API background checks).
* **Proven proxies:** Look for large companies running a similar model in a **different geography or adjacent vertical** (e.g., DoorDash validating the delivery model for Rappi in Latin America).
* **Long-term interest:** Founders often become passionate about a space **after the business starts working**, even in traditionally boring domains like tax software or payroll.
* **Scalability:** Pure software scales infinitely; be cautious of business models driven by **high-touch, linear human labor** (agencies, dev shops).
* **Fertile idea space:** An idea space is a high-level category of related ideas. Choose a fertile space with a **high baseline hit rate** (e.g., B2B vertical SaaS) so you can easily pivot into adjacent viable problems if your first attempt misses.

Traits That Look Bad but Are Actually Good

* **Ideas that are hard to get started (Schlep Blindness):** Problems involving tedious operational friction, heavy regulations, or difficult partner deals scare off most founders, **leaving massive market opportunities uncontested** (e.g., Stripe dealing with early bank negotiations).
* **Boring spaces:** Unsexy industries (e.g., payroll software like Gusto) have a **much higher hit rate** than consumer-facing "fun" apps; the day-to-day work of coding, debugging, and talking to users is identical regardless of how exciting the pitch sounds.
* **Crowded markets with bad products:** Markets with dozens of existing competitors where users are still dissatisfied signal **strong, validated demand paired with unsolved execution** (e.g., Dropbox launching into a market with 20 file storage tools by introducing OS-level background syncing).

How to Generate Startup Ideas

* **Organic discovery over brainstorming:** At least **70% of the top 100 YC companies** got their ideas organically; forcing ideas by sitting in a room typically generates tar pit ideas.
* **Long-term positioning:** Become a deep domain expert, work at an early-stage startup, or build things purely out of personal technical curiosity.
* **Recipe 1 (Highest hit rate):** Start with your **team's specific unfair advantage or domain expertise** to guarantee immediate founder-market fit.
* **Recipe 2:** Solve an **acute problem you personally encountered** from an unusual vantage point (e.g., VetCove modernizing supply ordering for veterinary clinics).
* **The Experience Audit:** Have every co-founder systematically review every prior job, internship, and life experience to identify **unique insights that others don't know** and problems only they could see.
* **Recipe 3:** Build things you personally wish existed, but deliberately verify there is **no hidden structural reason** why it hasn't succeeded before.
* **Recipe 4:** Capitalize on **recent behavioral or macro shocks** (e.g., remote-work collaboration tools post-2020).
* **Recipe 5:** Take a proven business model and build a **localized or verticalized variant** (e.g., Flexport for Latin America).
* **Recipe 6:** Choose a fertile, undigitized industry and **physically go interview participants and existing founders** to map out unsolved workflows (e.g., A to B visiting truck stops to identify the need for fuel cards).
* **Recipe 7:** Attack large, legacy industries that are **visibly broken and run on outdated workflows**.
* **Launch to validate:** Because early analysis can only take you so far, the single definitive test of an idea is to **launch an MVP and put it in front of real users**.
